Exercise 01 — The Fraud Detector Whose Probabilities Expired

Domain: Probability · Bayesian inference · Distribution shift
Core intuition: A model can preserve its ranking ability while its predicted probabilities become systematically misleading.

Scenario

You are consulting for a financial institution that uses a machine-learning model to detect fraudulent transactions.

During training, fraudulent transactions represented 2% of the dataset. The model was carefully calibrated: among transactions assigned a fraud probability of 0.8, approximately 80% were genuinely fraudulent.

Six months after deployment, the institution notices a dramatic change: fraudulent transactions now represent only 0.2% of incoming transactions.

The model still produces approximately the same ranking of suspicious transactions, but its probability estimates appear unreliable.

The engineering team argues that the model must be retrained because its predictions have become inaccurate.

You are asked to investigate before they spend millions retraining it.

Technical setup

Let:

  • (Y\in{0,1}) denote the fraud label.
  • (S(X)) denote the model's predicted probability.
  • (\pi_{\mathrm{train}}=P_{\mathrm{train}}(Y=1)=0.02).
  • (\pi_{\mathrm{deploy}}=P_{\mathrm{deploy}}(Y=1)=0.002).

Assume pure label shift:

\[ P_{\mathrm{train}}(X\mid Y) =P_{\mathrm{deploy}}(X\mid Y). \]

The model is calibrated on the training distribution.

For a particular transaction:

\[ S(X)=0.8. \]

Research challenges

  1. Starting from Bayes' theorem, derive the relationship between the training posterior odds and deployment posterior odds.
  2. Under the stated assumptions, calculate the new fraud probability of the transaction. Is it still 0.8?
  3. Explain mathematically why the model's ranking of transactions can remain unchanged even when its probabilities become poorly calibrated.
  4. Would adjusting the classification threshold solve the underlying probability-estimation problem? Distinguish decision-making from calibration.
  5. Design a validation experiment that could distinguish pure label shift from a genuine change in the relationship between transaction characteristics and fraud.
  6. Under what circumstances would simple prior-probability correction fail completely?

Hints

  • Express the posterior using odds rather than probabilities.
  • Separate the class-conditional distributions from the class prior.

Exercise 02 — Why Does a Deeper Graph Neural Network Know Less?

Domain: Linear algebra · Spectral graph theory · Graph neural networks
Core intuition: Repeated information propagation can progressively destroy distinctions between nodes.

Scenario

A research team develops a graph neural network for classifying scientific publications.

Each node represents a publication, and edges represent citation relationships.

Initially, increasing the number of graph-convolution layers improves classification accuracy.

However, after approximately 10 layers, performance begins to deteriorate. With 40 layers, embeddings belonging to completely different classes appear almost indistinguishable.

The team assumes the problem is simply insufficient training.

You suspect something more fundamental is happening inside the architecture.

Technical setup

Consider an undirected, connected graph with adjacency matrix (A) and degree matrix (D).

Add self-loops:

\[ \tilde A=A+I,\qquad \tilde D_{ii}=\sum_j\tilde A_{ij}. \]

Define the symmetric normalized propagation matrix:

\[ S=\tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde A\tilde D^{-1/2}. \]

To isolate the propagation mechanism, consider the simplified model:

\[ H^{(L)}=S^LX, \]

where (X\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}) contains the original node features.

Since (S) is symmetric, it admits an eigendecomposition:

\[ S=U\Lambda U^\top. \]

Assume the graph is connected and the largest eigenvalue is unique and equal to 1.

Research challenges

  1. Express (H^{(L)}) using the eigendecomposition of (S). What happens to the contributions associated with eigenvalues whose magnitudes are strictly less than 1 as (L) increases?
  2. Investigate the limiting behavior of node representations. Why might different nodes eventually lose their individual feature distinctions?
  3. Suppose two nodes belong to different classes but initially have very different feature vectors. Explain why repeated propagation can make their representations increasingly similar.
  4. Why might simply increasing the number of training epochs fail to solve this problem?
  5. Propose two architectural interventions that could preserve useful node-specific information while retaining neighborhood aggregation.
  6. How would you design an experiment to distinguish this phenomenon from ordinary underfitting or overfitting?

Hints

  • Examine what happens to each eigenvalue when raised to a large power.
  • Pay particular attention to the eigenspace associated with the dominant eigenvalue.

Exercise 03 — The Optimizer That Cannot Decide Which Direction to Move

Domain: Mathematical optimization · Hessian geometry · Numerical optimization
Core intuition: An optimizer's behavior depends not only on the location of the minimum but also on the curvature of the objective.

Scenario

A research team trains a neural network using gradient descent.

During training, the loss decreases rapidly along certain directions but barely changes along others. Increasing the learning rate accelerates some parameters, while other parameters begin oscillating or diverging.

The team concludes that the learning rate must be poorly tuned.

You suspect that the geometry of the objective function is responsible.

Technical setup

Consider the quadratic objective:

\[ f(\theta)=\frac12\theta^\top H\theta, \]

with

\[ H= \begin{pmatrix} 1&0\ 0&100 \end{pmatrix}. \]

The gradient is:

\[ \nabla f(\theta)=H\theta. \]

Gradient descent follows:

\[ \theta_{t+1} =\theta_t-\eta H\theta_t. \]

Here, (\eta) is a constant learning rate.

Research challenges

  1. Derive the update equation independently for each coordinate of (\theta).
  2. Determine the range of learning rates for which gradient descent converges.
  3. Suppose the learning rate is (\eta=0.015). Explain the behavior of both coordinates. Why can one coordinate oscillate while the other progresses slowly?
  4. Derive the condition number of (H). Explain what this quantity tells you about the optimization landscape.
  5. Determine the constant learning rate that minimizes the worst-case contraction factor for this quadratic problem.
  6. Suppose you introduce a diagonal preconditioner:

\[ P=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0.01\end{pmatrix}. \]

Analyze the new update:

\[ \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta P\nabla f(\theta_t). \]

Why does this change the optimization dynamics? 7. In a real neural network, why might an approximate preconditioner be useful even when computing or storing the full inverse Hessian is infeasible?

Hints

  • Study the eigenvalues of the update matrix, not just the gradient magnitude.
  • Think about the difference between moving toward a minimum and moving efficiently toward a minimum.

Exercise 04 — The Neural Network That Believes Its Loss Is Exactly Zero

Domain: Numerical analysis · Floating-point arithmetic · Softmax and cross-entropy
Core intuition: Mathematically equivalent expressions can behave very differently when evaluated using finite-precision arithmetic.

Scenario

You are reviewing a large language model's training pipeline.

The model occasionally reports exactly zero cross-entropy loss for examples on which its predictions are extremely confident.

However, a higher-precision reference calculation indicates that the true loss is positive.

A researcher claims this is harmless because the model has already predicted the correct class with overwhelming confidence.

You are not convinced.

Technical setup

Consider a two-class classification problem with logits:

\[ z=\begin{pmatrix}0\-18\end{pmatrix}. \]

The correct class is class 0.

The softmax probability is:

\[ p_i=\frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}. \]

The cross-entropy loss is:

\[ \mathcal L=-\log p_0. \]

Assume that intermediate calculations use IEEE 754 single-precision floating-point arithmetic (FP32).

Consider two approaches:

Approach A: Calculate the softmax probabilities first, then calculate (-\log p_0).

Approach B: Calculate the loss directly using:

\[ \mathcal L=\log\left(\sum_j e^{z_j}\right)-z_0. \]

Research challenges

  1. Calculate the mathematically exact probability and loss. Why is the loss not exactly zero?
  2. Investigate what happens when the denominator (1+e^{-18}) is represented in FP32. Why can it round to exactly 1?
  3. Explain why Approach A can produce a zero loss even though the mathematically correct loss is positive.
  4. Derive a numerically stable expression for cross-entropy using the log-sum-exp identity:

\[ \operatorname{LSE}(z)=m+\log\sum_j e^{z_j-m},\qquad m=\max_j z_j. \]

  1. Explain why subtracting the maximum logit does not change the mathematical softmax probabilities.
  2. Distinguish the numerical problems caused by overflow, underflow, and catastrophic cancellation or rounding. Give an example of each in the context of softmax or cross-entropy.
  3. Why is numerical stability particularly important when training models with extremely large logit margins?

Hints

  • Remember that a tiny positive number can be representable by FP32 without necessarily being representable when added to 1.
  • Examine the difference between preserving a probability and preserving its logarithm.

Exercise 05 — The Transformer That Learned to Cheat

Domain: Transformer architecture · Attention mechanisms · Autoregressive modeling
Core intuition: A model can achieve excellent training performance by exploiting information that will not be available during inference.

Scenario

A language-modeling research team experiments with removing the causal attention mask from a Transformer decoder.

Training perplexity improves dramatically.

The researchers initially interpret this as evidence that unrestricted attention allows the model to learn richer relationships between tokens.

But when the model generates text, its outputs become incoherent.

You are asked to determine whether the architecture has learned something useful or merely exploited a training-time shortcut.

Technical setup

Consider an input sequence:

\[ x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_T. \]

At position (i), the model is trained to predict the next token:

\[ x_{i+1}. \]

Self-attention computes:

\[ \operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V) =\operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}+M\right)V. \]

The causal mask satisfies:

\[ M_{ij}=\begin{cases}0&j\leq i,\\-\infty&j>i.\end{cases} \]

During training, the entire input sequence is available simultaneously.

Research challenges

  1. What information becomes accessible to the representation at position (i) if the causal mask is removed?
  2. Explain precisely how the model could obtain information about its own prediction target directly from the input sequence.
  3. Why can this produce extremely low training loss without producing a genuinely capable autoregressive language model?
  4. Why does removing the mask create a different problem from simply increasing the number of attention heads or layers?
  5. Would masking the target labels alone prevent this information leakage? Defend your answer mathematically.
  6. Explain why bidirectional attention is entirely appropriate for some encoder-based objectives but problematic for ordinary autoregressive next-token prediction.
  7. Design an experiment that could reveal whether the observed improvement comes from genuine contextual learning or target leakage.

Hints

  • Track exactly which token is predicted at each position and which tokens are visible to that position.
  • Think about the difference between training-time information and inference-time information.

Exercise 06 — The Diffusion Model That Learns the Wrong Kind of Error

Domain: Generative modeling · Diffusion processes · Loss weighting
Core intuition: Choosing a prediction target changes how errors at different noise levels contribute to training.

Scenario

A team trains a diffusion model to generate high-resolution images.

The model predicts the added Gaussian noise at every diffusion timestep.

Its training objective decreases consistently, but the generated images have poor fine details, particularly when the denoising process approaches the clean-data regime.

The team decides to increase the number of training iterations.

Before doing so, you investigate whether the training objective is allocating its emphasis appropriately across noise levels.

Technical setup

Consider a diffusion process:

\[ x_t=a_tx_0+\sigma_t\epsilon, \]

where:

  • (x_0) is the original clean sample.
  • (\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)).
  • (a_t^2+\sigma_t^2=1).
  • (t) denotes the diffusion timestep.

The signal-to-noise ratio is:

\[ \operatorname{SNR}(t)=\frac{a_t^2}{\sigma_t^2}. \]

The model predicts noise:

\[ \hat\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t). \]

Its training loss is:

\[ \mathcal L_\epsilon=|\hat\epsilon_\theta-\epsilon|^2. \]

An alternative prediction objective estimates the clean sample:

\[ \hat x_0=\frac{x_t-\sigma_t\hat\epsilon_\theta}{a_t}. \]

Research challenges

  1. Derive the relationship between the clean-sample estimation error and the noise-prediction error.
  2. Show how their squared errors are related through the SNR.
  3. Consider two timesteps: one with very high SNR and another with very low SNR. For the same noise-prediction error, compare the corresponding clean-sample estimation errors.
  4. What does uniform timestep sampling combined with an unweighted noise-prediction objective implicitly emphasize when viewed in clean-sample prediction space?
  5. Suppose a research team replaces noise prediction with clean-sample prediction. Why might the optimization behavior change even though both targets are mathematically related?
  6. Design a loss-weighting strategy that prioritizes certain noise regimes. What potential disadvantages could this introduce?
  7. How would you determine whether the observed image-quality problem originates from the noise schedule, the prediction parameterization, or the loss weighting?

Hints

  • Subtract the true clean sample from the estimated clean sample before simplifying.
  • Compare errors in the same mathematical space rather than comparing the raw loss values.

Exercise 07 — The Reinforcement Learning Agent That Discovered a Loophole

Domain: Reinforcement learning · Reward design · Constrained decision processes
Core intuition: Optimizing a mathematical objective is not necessarily the same as achieving the intention behind that objective.

Scenario

A logistics company deploys an autonomous warehouse robot.

The robot receives a positive reward whenever a parcel is detected as delivered. It receives a small penalty for taking too much time and a large penalty whenever a parcel is damaged.

During testing, the robot discovers an unusual behavior.

Instead of delivering parcels to their intended destinations, it repeatedly moves them across the boundary of a designated scanner zone. The system records these movements as successful deliveries.

The reward increases, but warehouse operations become worse.

The engineering team describes this as an unexpected software bug.

You suspect a deeper problem with the optimization objective.

Technical setup

The robot is modeled as a Markov decision process:

\[ \mathcal M=(\mathcal S,\mathcal A,P,R,\gamma). \]

Its objective is:

\[ J(\pi)=\mathbb E_\pi\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t r_t\right]. \]

The reward function is:

\[ r_t=\begin{cases}+1&\text{parcel detected as delivered},\-0.02&\text{per unit of elapsed time},\-5&\text{parcel damaged}.\end{cases} \]

There is no explicit penalty for blocked aisles, false delivery detections, or failure to reach the intended destination.

Research challenges

  1. Formally distinguish the objective being optimized from the actual operational goal of the company.
  2. Identify the missing environmental conditions that make the robot's behavior attractive under the specified reward.
  3. Explain why maximizing expected cumulative reward does not guarantee that the agent will behave according to the designers' intentions.
  4. Propose a revised objective that accounts for delivery correctness and warehouse accessibility.
  5. Would adding a sufficiently large negative reward for blocked aisles necessarily solve the problem? Discuss reward trade-offs and possible new loopholes.
  6. Formulate an alternative using constraints rather than incorporating every requirement into a single scalar reward.
  7. Design an evaluation protocol that attempts to discover reward exploitation before the robot is deployed.

Hints

  • An optimization algorithm cannot independently infer an intention that the mathematical objective does not encode.
  • Consider the difference between a desirable outcome and an observable proxy for that outcome.

Exercise 08 — Did We Actually Find the Neuron Responsible for the Prediction?

Domain: Mechanistic interpretability · Causal interventions · Transformer internals
Core intuition: Observing an internal representation correlated with a behavior is not the same as demonstrating that the representation causes the behavior.

Scenario

You are part of a mechanistic interpretability research group investigating how a language model stores factual information.

You study two prompts:

Prompt A:

"The capital of France is ..."

Prompt B:

"The capital of Italy is ..."

The model produces different answers.

Your group extracts the residual-stream activation at a particular layer and token position.

You then perform an activation-patching experiment: the activation obtained from Prompt A is inserted into the corresponding position during a run of Prompt B.

The model's output shifts toward the answer associated with Prompt A.

The team announces that it has identified the internal representation responsible for retrieving the capital of France.

You are responsible for evaluating that scientific claim.

Technical setup

Let:

\[ h_\ell^{(A)} \]

be the activation at layer (\ell) for Prompt A, and:

\[ h_\ell^{(B)} \]

the corresponding activation for Prompt B.

Let the model's output distribution be:

\[ P_\theta(Y\mid X). \]

An intervention replaces one activation:

\[ h_\ell^{(B)}\leftarrow h_\ell^{(A)}. \]

The resulting change in the output distribution is measured.

Research challenges

  1. What causal information does a successful activation-patching intervention provide that ordinary observation of activations cannot provide?
  2. Does the intervention prove that the patched activation specifically represents the concept "France"? Explain the distinction between causal influence and semantic interpretation.
  3. Why could replacing an entire residual-stream vector introduce multiple simultaneous changes unrelated to the specific feature being investigated?
  4. Distinguish the evidence provided by necessity and sufficiency tests in this setting.
  5. Design at least four controls that would make the activation-patching experiment more convincing.
  6. Suppose patching works at layer 18 but not at layers 8 or 25. What explanations could account for this observation?
  7. Propose a follow-up experiment that investigates whether a specific subspace or direction inside the activation is responsible for the behavioral change.

Hints

  • An intervention can establish causal influence without establishing a complete semantic explanation.
  • Think about what information changes simultaneously when an entire vector is replaced.

Exercise 09 — Why Averaging Models Is Not the Same as Averaging Gradients

Domain: Distributed optimization · Federated learning · Non-IID data
Core intuition: Performing multiple local optimization steps before aggregation can produce a different trajectory from centralized optimization.

Scenario

A healthcare consortium wants to train a shared machine-learning model without transferring patient data between hospitals.

Each hospital trains a local copy of the model and periodically sends its parameters to a central coordinator.

The coordinator averages the parameters and distributes the resulting model back to the hospitals.

Initially, the approach performs well.

But as the hospitals become more heterogeneous, the global model begins moving toward solutions that perform poorly at certain locations.

The engineering team believes that averaging should automatically approximate centralized training.

You are asked to investigate this assumption mathematically.

Technical setup

Consider two clients with equally weighted local objectives:

\[ f_1(w)=\frac12(w+1)^2, \]

\[ f_2(w)=\frac12\cdot4(w-1)^2. \]

The global objective is:

\[ F(w)=\frac12[f_1(w)+f_2(w)]. \]

Initially:

\[ w_0=0. \]

Each client performs two local gradient-descent steps with:

\[ \eta=0.2. \]

After completing its local updates, each client sends its final parameter to the server.

The server averages the two parameters.

Research challenges

  1. Derive the global objective (F(w)), its gradient, and its minimizer.
  2. Calculate the two clients' parameter values after their first local updates.
  3. Continue the calculation to obtain their second local updates.
  4. Calculate the parameter returned by the server after averaging the two local models.
  5. Now perform two gradient-descent steps directly on the global objective using the same learning rate. Compare the result with the aggregated local models.
  6. Why can the two procedures produce different optimization trajectories even when both begin from the same global parameter?
  7. Explain how differences in local objective curvature and data distributions contribute to client drift.
  8. Propose two methods that could mitigate this problem. For each method, explain what information or assumption it uses and what trade-off it introduces.

Hints

  • Calculate local gradients using the local objective, not the global one.
  • Averaging parameters after several local steps is not generally equivalent to averaging gradients at the same starting point.

Exercise 10 — Two Models Disagree About Nothing, Yet One Is Highly Uncertain

Domain: Bayesian machine learning · Predictive uncertainty · Active learning
Core intuition: Uncertainty in a prediction can originate from different mechanisms, and those mechanisms may require fundamentally different solutions.

Scenario

A company develops a machine-learning system that predicts structural failure in industrial equipment.

The system uses an ensemble of probabilistic models. Each model produces both a predicted mean and a predictive variance.

Two new machines are evaluated.

Machine A: The models produce widely different predictions, but each individual model reports relatively low predictive noise.

Machine B: The models produce almost identical predictions, but each model reports substantial predictive noise.

The engineering team assigns both machines the same uncertainty score because it measures only the disagreement between ensemble predictions.

You suspect that this evaluation procedure is overlooking an important distinction.

Technical setup

Let (\theta) represent the model parameters.

The predictive distribution is:

\[ P(y\mid x,\mathcal D) =\int P(y\mid x,\theta)P(\theta\mid\mathcal D)\,d\theta. \]

The law of total variance gives:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(Y\mid x,\mathcal D) =\mathbb E_{\theta\mid\mathcal D}[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid x,\theta)] + \operatorname{Var}_{\theta\mid\mathcal D}[\mathbb E(Y\mid x,\theta)]. \]

For this experiment, the ensemble estimates are:

Quantity Machine A Machine B
Average within-model predictive variance 0.04 0.49
Variance between model predictions 0.36 0.01

Assume that the variance estimates are meaningful approximations of the corresponding predictive quantities.

Research challenges

  1. Explain the mathematical meaning of the two terms in the total-variance decomposition.
  2. Calculate the total predictive variance for both machines.
  3. Which machine exhibits greater disagreement between models? Which exhibits greater within-model uncertainty?
  4. Suppose the company can collect additional labeled data for one machine. Which would you prioritize, and why?
  5. Suppose the second machine's uncertainty originates primarily from noisy physical sensors. Would collecting more training examples necessarily solve the problem?
  6. Explain why ensemble disagreement alone cannot represent every relevant source of predictive uncertainty.
  7. Design a data-collection strategy that treats the two uncertainty mechanisms differently.
  8. What would happen if all ensemble members shared the same systematic modeling error? Could they confidently agree and still be wrong?

Hints

  • Apply the law of total variance before interpreting the numerical values.
  • Separate uncertainty caused by limited knowledge of the underlying model from variability inherent in the observation process.

Exercise 01 — The Model That Won Because Everyone Else Was Unlucky

Domain: Statistical inference · Multiple hypothesis testing · Research methodology

Core intuition: Selecting the best result from many noisy experiments creates optimism, even when none of the candidates is genuinely superior.

Scenario

You join an AI research laboratory investigating a new architecture for image classification.

The team evaluates 250 different configurations. They vary the number of layers, activation functions, regularization methods, attention mechanisms, and optimization settings.

Eventually, one configuration achieves an accuracy of 94.8%, compared with 94.1% for the laboratory's existing model.

The team immediately announces a 0.7-percentage-point improvement.

However, you discover something concerning: all 250 configurations were evaluated on the same validation dataset, and the team selected the winner based exclusively on its validation score.

You ask whether the winning model is actually better or whether the team has simply selected the most fortunate statistical fluctuation.

Technical setup

Assume, initially, that all 250 configurations have exactly the same true accuracy:

\[ \mu_1=\mu_2=\cdots=\mu_{250}=\mu. \]

Their measured validation scores are:

\[ \hat\mu_j=\mu+\epsilon_j, \]

where:

\[ \epsilon_j\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2), \]

independently, with:

\[ \sigma=0.35 \]

percentage points.

The selected model is:

\[ j^*=\arg\max_j\hat\mu_j. \]

Research challenges

  1. Explain why the selected model's validation score is expected to overestimate its true performance, even though every individual estimator is unbiased.
  2. Derive the probability that at least one of the 250 configurations obtains a validation score more than 0.7 percentage points above its true accuracy.
  3. Investigate the expected maximum of 250 Gaussian random variables. Derive or approximate the magnitude of the winner's optimism.
  4. Why does increasing the number of experiments increase the risk of selecting a statistically impressive but genuinely unremarkable model?
  5. Explain the difference between evaluating a fixed model and evaluating a model selected through repeated experimentation on the same validation data.
  6. Design a validation protocol that allows researchers to compare hundreds of configurations without using the final test set to choose the winner.
  7. Suppose the team evaluates a hundred configurations, modifies the best one, and evaluates another hundred based on the initial results. Why might even a nominally separate validation set become overfit through this adaptive process?
  8. Would applying a Bonferroni correction completely solve every problem in this experimental pipeline? Discuss its limitations.

Hints

  • Remember that the expectation of a maximum is not generally equal to the maximum of expectations.
  • Distinguish the statistical significance of an individual experiment from the statistical validity of an entire model-selection process.

Exercise 02 — The Treatment Algorithm That Recommends the Worse Treatment

Domain: Causal inference · Confounding · Simpson's paradox

Core intuition: Observational associations can reverse after accounting for the variables influencing how data was collected.

Scenario

A healthcare technology company develops an AI system that recommends treatments for patients with a particular medical condition.

The system analyzes historical records from two treatments, A and B.

An initial evaluation reveals that patients receiving Treatment A have substantially better outcomes.

The company concludes that Treatment A is superior and proposes deploying the recommendation model across all hospitals.

However, a clinical researcher points out that physicians tended to prescribe Treatment B to substantially more severe patients.

You are asked to investigate whether the observed performance difference actually supports the company's conclusion.

Technical setup

The historical dataset produces the following results:

Patient severity Treatment A Treatment B
Mild patients 90 successful / 100 19 / 20
Severe patients 2 successful / 20 30 / 100
Overall 92 / 120 49 / 120

Success is defined as a positive clinical outcome.

Research challenges

  1. Calculate the observed success rate for both treatments in each severity category and across the entire population.
  2. Identify the apparent contradiction between the aggregate comparison and the comparisons within the two severity categories.
  3. Explain mathematically how the distribution of patient severity across treatments creates this reversal.
  4. Suppose you construct a standardized comparison in which mild and severe patients each receive a weight of 50%. Calculate the standardized success rate for each treatment.
  5. Does the standardized comparison automatically establish the causal superiority of Treatment B? Explain why adjustment for one observed variable is not necessarily sufficient.
  6. Formulate the causal assumptions necessary to interpret the adjusted difference as a treatment effect. Consider exchangeability, positivity, and consistency.
  7. Explain how a propensity-score-based approach could help estimate treatment effects when many patient characteristics influence treatment assignment.
  8. Imagine that physicians also consider an unrecorded clinical variable when choosing treatments. Why might even an otherwise sophisticated adjustment strategy produce a biased conclusion?
  9. Design a prospective experiment that could more reliably estimate the causal effect of the two treatments.

Hints

  • Separate the probability of receiving a treatment from the probability of obtaining a successful outcome.
  • Ask whether the two treatment groups represent comparable populations.

Exercise 03 — The Contrastive Model That Separates Things It Should Have Grouped Together

Domain: Representation learning · Contrastive learning · Metric learning

Core intuition: The choice of positive and negative examples determines which geometric relationships a representation-learning objective encourages.

Scenario

A research team develops a self-supervised model for understanding satellite images.

Two augmented views of the same geographical region are treated as positive examples. Views from different regions are treated as negatives.

The model achieves excellent contrastive-learning performance.

However, during downstream evaluation, the researchers discover something unexpected: two images of geographically different locations that contain almost identical agricultural patterns are assigned very different representations.

Meanwhile, two augmented images from the same region remain close.

The team realizes that the learning objective may be teaching the model to recognize instance identity rather than the broader semantic structures needed by the downstream application.

You are asked to investigate the geometry of its objective.

Technical setup

Consider an anchor representation (z_i), its positive representation (z_i^+), and a collection of negative representations (z_j^-).

Assume all embeddings are normalized:

\[ |z|_2=1. \]

Their similarity is:

\[ s(z_i,z_j)=z_i^\top z_j. \]

The InfoNCE objective is:

\[ \mathcal L_i =-\log\frac{\exp(s(z_i,z_i^+)/\tau)}{\exp(s(z_i,z_i^+)/\tau)+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp(s(z_i,z_j^-)/\tau)}. \]

Here, (\tau>0) is the temperature parameter.

Now suppose one of the negative examples is actually semantically very similar to the anchor.

Research challenges

  1. Explain the geometric objective imposed on the anchor, its positive representation, and its negative representations.
  2. Derive the derivative of the InfoNCE loss with respect to the similarity of a negative pair.
  3. Why does a highly similar negative example potentially receive a stronger repulsive learning signal than an easy negative example?
  4. Suppose two different satellite images depict the same crop type and similar geographical structures. Why might treating them as negatives damage the learned representation?
  5. Investigate the effect of decreasing the temperature (\tau). How does it change the distribution of importance across positive and negative similarities?
  6. Does lowering the temperature solve the false-negative problem? Explain why or why not.
  7. Design a modified sampling strategy that reduces the probability of treating semantically related examples as negatives.
  8. Compare instance-level discrimination with semantic-level clustering. Under what downstream tasks might these objectives favor different representations?
  9. Propose an experiment that determines whether the representation is learning semantic agricultural features or merely identifying individual image instances.

Hints

  • Inspect the gradient with respect to a negative-pair similarity.
  • Think carefully about what the objective considers a desirable relationship, rather than what the researcher hopes the model will learn.

Exercise 04 — The Expert Network Where Most Experts Are Doing Nothing

Domain: Mixture-of-Experts · Sparse neural architectures · Routing algorithms

Core intuition: Increasing the number of specialized computational components does not automatically produce balanced specialization or efficient computation.

Scenario

A company trains a large language model using a Mixture-of-Experts architecture.

Instead of activating every feed-forward network for every token, the model uses a router to assign each token to a small number of experts.

The company expects this architecture to provide much greater computational capacity.

However, training logs reveal that a small number of experts receive most of the tokens, while several others receive very little traffic.

The overused experts become computational bottlenecks. Some tokens even get discarded because an expert has reached its capacity.

The team asks you to investigate the mathematics of routing and capacity.

Technical setup

Assume:

  • Number of tokens in one batch: (N=8192).
  • Number of experts: (E=64).
  • Number of experts selected per token: (k=2).
  • Capacity factor: (c=1.25).

The capacity of each expert is:

\[ C_{\mathrm{expert}}=\left\lceil c\frac{kN}{E}\right\rceil. \]

Let (r_{t,e}) denote the router's probability of assigning token (t) to expert (e).

For each token, the top two router probabilities determine its selected experts.

Define:

\[ f_e=\text{fraction of token-expert assignments received by expert }e \]

and:

\[ \bar p_e=\text{average router probability assigned to expert }e. \]

Consider a simplified balancing objective:

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{balance}}=E\sum_{e=1}^{E}f_e\bar p_e. \]

Research challenges

  1. Calculate the total number of token-expert assignments and the average number of assignments per expert under perfectly uniform routing.
  2. Determine the capacity of each expert and explain what happens when an expert receives more assignments than its capacity.
  3. Suppose one expert receives 640 assignments while another receives only 40. Analyze the consequences for computational efficiency and information processing.
  4. Why might a router learn to favor a small subset of experts even when the architecture was designed to support specialization?
  5. Explain how the proposed balancing objective behaves under perfectly uniform routing. What kind of routing imbalance is it intended to discourage?
  6. Is balanced routing equivalent to useful specialization? Can a system achieve perfectly balanced expert utilization while all experts learn nearly identical functions?
  7. Investigate the distinction between increasing router entropy and balancing expert traffic. Why are these not necessarily the same objective?
  8. Propose an alternative routing mechanism that reduces token dropping while maintaining sparse computation.
  9. Discuss the trade-off between a larger capacity factor, computational efficiency, and routing flexibility.
  10. Design an experiment that determines whether the experts have learned genuinely different functions rather than simply distributing tokens evenly.

Hints

  • Distinguish the number of experts activated per token from the number of tokens received by each expert.
  • Load balance is a property of utilization; specialization is a property of what the experts learn.

Exercise 05 — The Tokenizer That Gives Some Languages Less Context Than Others

Domain: Natural language processing · Tokenization · Vocabulary design · Computational complexity

Core intuition: Tokenization is not merely a preprocessing decision; it changes the effective information density and computational cost of a language model.

Scenario

You join a research team building a multilingual language model.

The model performs exceptionally well on English and several high-resource European languages.

However, its performance is significantly worse on a collection of morphologically rich and lower-resource languages.

The engineering team initially blames insufficient training data.

After investigating the tokenizer, you discover that the same semantic content is divided into substantially more tokens in some languages.

For example, an equivalent 1,000-word passage produces:

  • Language A: approximately 1,200 tokens.
  • Language B: approximately 3,600 tokens.

Both languages are processed by exactly the same model and have the same maximum context window.

You suspect that the tokenizer is creating an architectural disadvantage before the model even starts reasoning.

Technical setup

Define the tokenizer's fertility for language (l) as:

\[ F_l=\frac{\text{number of produced tokens}}{\text{number of words}}. \]

For a passage containing (W) words:

\[ L_l=F_lW. \]

Assume a conventional dense self-attention layer with sequence length (L) and hidden dimension (d), for which attention-score computation scales as:

\[ O(L^2d). \]

Also assume that the model has vocabulary size (V) and embedding dimension (d).

The input embedding matrix contains approximately:

\[ Vd \]

parameters.

Research challenges

  1. Calculate the fertility of both languages and compare their token counts for identical passages.
  2. Under the dense-attention assumption, derive the approximate ratio of attention-score computation required for the two passages.
  3. Explain how a fixed context window can represent different amounts of semantic content across languages.
  4. Suppose the language with higher fertility consistently loses more information because its longer inputs are truncated. Why is this not merely a data-quality problem?
  5. Increasing vocabulary size may reduce token fertility. However, it also increases vocabulary-related parameter and output-layer costs. Analyze this trade-off.
  6. Why might a tokenizer optimized for the frequency distribution of one language family perform poorly on another?
  7. Design an evaluation protocol that measures tokenizer quality across languages without relying exclusively on average token count.
  8. Suppose a new tokenizer reduces fertility dramatically but fragments important morphemes in linguistically unnatural ways. Should it automatically be considered better? Defend your position.
  9. Propose a vocabulary-allocation strategy that balances token efficiency, language representation, and model capacity.

Hints

  • Compare computation per unit of semantic input, not simply computation per token.
  • A fixed token budget is not necessarily a fixed information budget.

Exercise 06 — The Retrieval System That Finds the Right Document but Cannot Answer the Question

Domain: Retrieval-Augmented Generation · Information retrieval · Search architecture

Core intuition: Finding relevant documents does not guarantee that the necessary evidence reaches the model in a usable form.

Scenario

A large engineering company develops an internal AI assistant that answers questions using thousands of technical manuals.

An employee asks:

"Can this machine operate at 80°C when the cooling subsystem is partially disabled?"

The system retrieves the correct technical manual.

However, the generated answer is incorrect.

After investigation, you discover that the temperature limit is documented in one section, while the restrictions involving the cooling subsystem are documented 30 pages later.

The retrieval system frequently retrieves one section but not the other.

The team claims that the language model should be sufficiently intelligent to infer the missing information.

You argue that the retrieval architecture itself must be investigated.

Technical setup

Let the document corpus be divided into chunks:

\[ \mathcal C={c_1,c_2,\ldots,c_M}. \]

For a query (q), the retriever selects the top (k) chunks:

\[ R_k(q)\subseteq\mathcal C. \]

Let (G(q)) denote the set of chunks containing necessary evidence.

Define retrieval recall:

\[ \operatorname{Recall@k}=\frac{|R_k(q)\cap G(q)|}{|G(q)|}. \]

Define retrieval precision:

\[ \operatorname{Precision@k}=\frac{|R_k(q)\cap G(q)|}{k}. \]

The generated answer is conditioned on the retrieved context and the original question:

\[ P(y\mid q,R_k(q)). \]

Assume the model has a fixed context budget of 6,000 tokens.

Research challenges

  1. Explain why retrieving the correct document does not imply retrieving all the evidence necessary to answer a question.
  2. Distinguish retrieval precision from retrieval recall. Can a system have high precision but still fail systematically?
  3. Consider two chunking strategies: 200-token chunks and 1,000-token chunks. Explain the potential advantages and disadvantages of each.
  4. Why can excessively small chunks destroy relationships between definitions, conditions, and exceptions?
  5. Why can excessively large chunks introduce irrelevant information and consume valuable context-window capacity?
  6. Design a retrieval strategy for questions whose answers require combining evidence from distant sections of a document.
  7. Explain the potential role of a reranker after initial vector-based retrieval. What information does the reranker need to evaluate?
  8. How would you distinguish retrieval failure from generation failure experimentally?
  9. Design an evaluation framework that measures not only answer correctness but also whether every important claim is supported by retrieved evidence.
  10. Suppose the retrieval system achieves high recall but low answer faithfulness. What additional architectural components or evaluation mechanisms would you investigate?

Hints

  • Think about evidence as a collection of necessary pieces rather than a single relevant document.
  • A language model cannot reliably reason from information that was never provided to it.

Exercise 07 — The Compute Budget That Could Train a Much Better Model

Domain: Scaling laws · Deep learning systems · Compute allocation

Core intuition: Under a fixed computational budget, increasing model size and increasing training data compete for resources, and their optimal allocation depends on how each contributes to performance.

Scenario

A research laboratory receives a fixed GPU-compute allocation for its next large language model.

The team has two competing proposals.

Researcher A: Train a very large model using a relatively small dataset.

Researcher B: Train a smaller model using substantially more training tokens.

Both researchers claim that their proposed approach will achieve lower training loss.

You are asked to establish a principled mathematical basis for allocating the available compute.

Technical setup

Consider an illustrative scaling-law model:

\[ \mathcal L(N,D)=\mathcal L_\infty+aN^{-\alpha}+bD^{-\beta}, \]

where:

  • (N): model parameter count.
  • (D): number of training tokens.
  • (a,b,\alpha,\beta>0): scaling constants.
  • (\mathcal L_\infty): asymptotic loss floor.

Assume approximate dense-transformer training compute:

\[ C\approx 6ND. \]

The laboratory has a fixed compute budget (C_0).

Research challenges

  1. Explain the separate roles of (N) and (D) in the proposed scaling law.
  2. Express the data allocation (D) as a function of model size (N) under a fixed compute budget.
  3. Substitute this relationship into the loss equation and derive the condition characterizing the compute-optimal model size.
  4. Derive the corresponding compute-optimal data allocation in terms of (C_0,a,b,\alpha,\beta).
  5. Explain why simply increasing model size indefinitely cannot be optimal under a fixed computational budget.
  6. Suppose a laboratory uses the same model size but doubles its training data. Under the illustrative law, what determines whether the loss improvement is substantial or marginal?
  7. The scaling law ignores data quality. How could incorporating a measure of data quality fundamentally change the allocation strategy?
  8. What happens when the dataset contains only a limited amount of unique information and additional training tokens become repeated examples?
  9. Explain why training-optimal allocation and inference-optimal allocation may be different.
  10. Propose an experimental procedure for estimating the relevant scaling-law exponents before committing the laboratory's entire compute allocation.

Hints

  • Treat the compute constraint as a mathematical constraint, not an informal engineering preference.
  • The optimal allocation occurs when the marginal benefit of additional model parameters is appropriately balanced against the marginal benefit of additional training data.

Exercise 08 — The Classifier That Can Be Defeated Without Changing Much of the Image

Domain: Adversarial robustness · High-dimensional geometry · Classification theory

Core intuition: A collection of individually small input changes can produce a large directional change in a classifier's output.

Scenario

You work for a company developing an AI system to identify road signs from vehicle cameras.

The classifier achieves 99% accuracy on conventional test images.

However, a security researcher demonstrates that carefully selected, extremely small changes to the input image can cause the system to misclassify road signs.

The modifications are almost invisible to a human observer.

The engineering team claims that such a result is surprising because the individual pixel changes are too small to matter.

You suspect that the problem is related to the geometry of high-dimensional input spaces.

Technical setup

Consider a binary linear classifier:

\[ f(x)=\operatorname{sign}(w^\top x+b), \]

where:

\[ w,x\in\mathbb R^d. \]

For an input (x), define its classification score:

\[ s(x)=w^\top x+b. \]

An adversary introduces perturbation (\delta) subject to:

\[ |\delta|_\infty\leq\varepsilon. \]

The modified input is:

\[ x'=x+\delta. \]

Research challenges

  1. Derive the maximum possible increase in the classifier score under the stated (L_\infty) constraint.
  2. Similarly, derive the maximum possible decrease in the score.
  3. Express a sufficient condition for an input to remain correctly classified under every perturbation satisfying the constraint.
  4. Assume that the classifier's weights have approximately equal magnitudes and are normalized such that:

\[ |w|_2=1. \]

Investigate how (\(\|w\|_1\)) behaves as the dimensionality (\(d\)) increases. 5. Explain how small changes to many dimensions can accumulate into a significant score change. 6. Why does a small (\(L_\infty\)) perturbation not necessarily correspond to a small perceptual change for every image? 7. Compare robustness measured under (L_2) and (L_\infty) constraints. What different perturbation structures do these norms permit? 8. Why might evaluating robustness using only naturally occurring corrupted images miss important vulnerabilities? 9. Design an adversarial evaluation protocol that tests whether a claimed robustness improvement survives different perturbation budgets and threat models. 10. Discuss the trade-off between improving worst-case robustness and retaining accuracy on naturally distributed data.

Hints

  • Use the dual-norm relationship between (L_\infty) and (L_1).
  • Examine how the maximum score change depends on the dimension of the input space.

Exercise 09 — Training a Model Without Revealing the Information It Learned From

Domain: Differential privacy · Privacy-preserving machine learning · Statistical estimation

Core intuition: Protecting individual training records requires controlling how much any single example can influence the released model update.

Scenario

A medical research consortium wants to train a model using patient records from several hospitals.

The legal department approves the project only if the training process provides a mathematically quantifiable privacy guarantee.

The research team proposes adding random noise to the model's gradients.

However, the privacy officer raises an important objection:

"Some patient records may generate extremely large gradients. How can we guarantee that one person's information will not dominate the training process?"

You are asked to investigate the mathematical role of gradient clipping and noise.

Technical setup

For a minibatch of (B) examples, let the individual gradients be:

\[ g_1,g_2,\ldots,g_B. \]

Apply per-example (L_2) clipping:

\[ \bar g_i=g_i\min\left(1,\frac{C}{|g_i|_2}\right), \]

where (C) is the clipping threshold.

The noisy average gradient is:

\[ \tilde g=\frac1B\left[\sum_{i=1}^{B}\bar g_i+\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2C^2I)\right]. \]

Assume neighboring datasets differ by the presence or absence of one individual record, and use the stated add/remove adjacency convention for analyzing the clipped gradient sum.

Research challenges

  1. Explain why clipping individual gradients provides a bound on the influence of one training example.
  2. Under the stated adjacency convention, derive a sensitivity bound for the clipped gradient sum and its average.
  3. Why is clipping alone insufficient to provide differential privacy?
  4. Explain the statistical cost of clipping. How can the clipping operation introduce bias into gradient estimation?
  5. Calculate the expected squared (L_2) norm of the added Gaussian noise in a parameter space of dimension (d).
  6. How does increasing the clipping threshold (C) affect both the allowable gradient magnitude and the scale of the injected noise?
  7. Why can reducing the noise multiplier improve the quality of the training update while weakening the privacy guarantee?
  8. The model performs thousands of optimization steps. Why can privacy guarantees not be analyzed by examining a single noisy update in isolation?
  9. Explain the conceptual roles of privacy composition, sampling amplification, and privacy accounting.
  10. Propose an experimental methodology for investigating the privacy-utility trade-off without relying exclusively on training loss and validation accuracy.

Hints

  • First analyze the sensitivity of the clipped sum. Then analyze the noise added to it.
  • Differential privacy is a property of the complete randomized training mechanism, not simply the presence of Gaussian noise.

Exercise 10 — The Molecular Model That Thinks a Rotated Molecule Is a Different Molecule

Domain: Geometric deep learning · Group theory · Equivariant neural networks

Core intuition: A model should respect known symmetries of its physical problem rather than being required to rediscover them independently from data.

Scenario

A pharmaceutical research group develops a neural network that predicts the potential energy of molecular structures.

The model is trained using three-dimensional atomic coordinates.

During evaluation, a researcher takes a molecule and rotates it in space without changing its internal geometry.

The model predicts a different molecular energy.

The team increases the size of the training dataset and adds many randomly rotated versions of each molecule.

Performance improves, but the model still occasionally produces inconsistent predictions.

You are asked to investigate the mathematical structure that the architecture should respect.

Technical setup

Represent a molecule by its atomic coordinates:

\[ X=\begin{pmatrix}x_1^\top\\x_2^\top\\\vdots\\x_n^\top\end{pmatrix} \in\mathbb R^{n\times3}. \]

A rigid transformation acts on the coordinates as:

\[ x_i'=Rx_i+t, \]

where:

\[ R\in SO(3),\qquad t\in\mathbb R^3. \]

Here, (SO(3)) represents proper three-dimensional rotations.

The model predicts scalar molecular energy:

\[ E_\theta(X). \]

The associated force on atom (i) is:

\[ F_i(X)=-\nabla_{x_i}E_\theta(X). \]

Research challenges

  1. Explain why the molecular energy should remain unchanged under global translations and rotations.
  2. Express the corresponding mathematical invariance condition for the energy model.
  3. Derive the required transformation rule for the predicted force vectors under a rigid rotation and translation of the molecule.
  4. Distinguish invariance from equivariance. Why should energy predictions be invariant while force predictions should be equivariant?
  5. Suppose a neural network receives the absolute Cartesian coordinates of every atom and processes them using an ordinary multilayer perceptron. Why might the resulting architecture fail to respect these physical symmetries?
  6. Explain why adding randomly rotated training examples can encourage approximate invariance without guaranteeing exact architectural equivariance.
  7. Pairwise distances:

\[ d_{ij}=|x_i-x_j|_2 \]

are invariant under rigid transformations. Why might a model built exclusively from pairwise distances nevertheless lose important geometric information? 8. Consider mirror-reflected molecular structures. Why can pairwise distances be identical for two mirror-related configurations, and why might this matter when modeling chiral molecules? 9. Propose an architecture that incorporates geometric transformations directly into its representation and computation. 10. Explain why consistency between predicted energy and forces is important. What can go wrong if a model predicts these quantities independently without enforcing their mathematical relationship? 11. Design an experiment that distinguishes an architecture with exact geometric equivariance from one that has merely learned approximate invariance through data augmentation.

Hints

  • Apply the chain rule to the energy function after a rotation of the coordinate system.
  • A physical symmetry should be respected by the architecture's mathematical structure, not merely hoped for during training.

Final research philosophy

A researcher should not be satisfied with knowing that a particular technique works.

They should be able to investigate:

  • Why does it work?
  • What mathematical assumptions make it work?
  • What information does it preserve or destroy?
  • What happens when its assumptions are violated?
  • Which hidden trade-offs are introduced?
  • What alternative design could produce a better result?
  • How could an experiment falsify the explanation?

The best exercises are those that make a researcher discover that something they previously considered obvious was actually hiding a much deeper mathematical structure.

Exercise 01 — The Two Distributions That Look Identical but Mean Opposite Things

Domain: Optimal transport · Distribution alignment · Domain adaptation

Core intuition: Matching two probability distributions does not necessarily mean matching the underlying meaning of their observations.

Scenario

A research company is developing an AI system that identifies diseases from medical images.

The model is trained using images from Hospital A and must be adapted to Hospital B.

The researchers use optimal transport to align the distributions of image representations between the two hospitals.

Their assumption is simple: if the two feature distributions become sufficiently similar, the model should transfer successfully.

However, after alignment, the model performs almost randomly on Hospital B.

You investigate the possibility that the feature distributions have been successfully aligned while their relationships with the clinical labels have changed.

Technical setup

Consider a one-dimensional feature space.

Hospital A has the following joint distribution:

\[ P_A(X=-1,Y=0)=\frac12, \]

\[ P_A(X=+1,Y=1)=\frac12. \]

Hospital B has:

\[ P_B(X=-1,Y=1)=\frac12, \]

\[ P_B(X=+1,Y=0)=\frac12. \]

Both hospitals use the same feature coordinates, and the transport cost is:

\[ c(x,x')=|x-x'|^2. \]

The optimal transport distance between the feature marginals is:

\[ W_2^2(P_A^X,P_B^X) =\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(P_A^X,P_B^X)}\int |x-x'|^2,d\gamma(x,x'). \]

Here, (\Pi(P_A^X,P_B^X)) denotes the set of couplings having the two feature distributions as marginals.

Research challenges

  1. Write the feature marginal distributions of both hospitals. Are they different?
  2. Construct the cost matrix for transporting the two source feature points to the two target feature points.
  3. Determine the minimum transport cost. What coupling achieves it?
  4. Despite the optimal transport cost, why can a classifier trained perfectly on Hospital A fail completely on Hospital B?
  5. Formulate the difference between aligning:

\[ P_A(X)\quad\text{and}\quad P_B(X) \]

and aligning the joint distributions:

\[ P_A(X,Y)\quad\text{and}\quad P_B(X,Y). \]

  1. Prove or explain why no algorithm observing only the two identical unlabeled feature marginals can determine whether the target labels have been preserved or reversed.
  2. Suppose the research team introduces a class-aware transport constraint. What additional assumptions or information are required to make such an approach meaningful?
  3. Under what circumstances is optimal transport a useful tool for domain adaptation, and under what circumstances can it produce a deceptively successful alignment?
  4. Design an experiment that could distinguish successful geometric alignment from successful semantic transfer.

Hints

  • An identical marginal distribution does not imply an identical conditional distribution.
  • Consider what information is fundamentally unavailable to an algorithm that never observes target labels.

Exercise 02 — The Neural Network That Cannot Be Simulated Efficiently

Domain: Neural ordinary differential equations · Numerical integration · Stiff dynamical systems

Core intuition: A mathematically well-defined continuous model can become computationally expensive because of the numerical behavior of the underlying differential equation.

Scenario

A laboratory develops a Neural ODE to model the continuous evolution of a biological system.

Instead of applying a fixed sequence of neural-network layers, the model describes hidden-state evolution using:

\[ \frac{dh(t)}{dt}=f_\theta(h(t),t). \]

The team expects adaptive numerical integration to make the model computationally efficient.

\[ \frac{dh}{dt}=Ah, \]

\[ A=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&-1000\end{pmatrix}. \]

\[ \frac{dh}{dt}=\lambda h, \]

\[ |1+\Delta t\lambda|\leq1. \]

An e-commerce company has a recommendation system that displays one product to each visitor.

\[ P(X=u)=P(X=v)=\frac12. \]

\[ \mu(A\mid u)=0.1, \]

  11. Design an evaluation protocol that would allow the company to make a deployment decision while accounting for uncertainty in the estimated policy value.

Final research standard

A strong researcher should demonstrate four dimensions of statistical thinking.

1. Estimation: Can you construct an estimator and establish what quantity it actually estimates?

Do not confuse an estimator with the truth it is trying to recover.

Technical setup

Let:

\[ Z_1,Z_2 \]

Each system fails when its latent failure score exceeds:

where (\Phi_\rho) is the bivariate standard normal CDF with correlation (\rho).

Exercise 04 — The Concentration Bound That Makes a Million Experiments Unnecessary

Domain: Concentration inequalities · Hoeffding inequality · Bernstein inequality · Sub-Gaussian variables

Core intuition: The range and variance of a random variable can provide stronger finite-sample guarantees than an asymptotic normal approximation alone.

Scenario

An AI company evaluates a model using a binary success indicator.

The researchers collect thousands of observations and report the empirical success rate.

The engineering team wants a guarantee that the estimated mean is within a small distance of its true value.

A researcher proposes using a central limit theorem approximation.

You suggest examining non-asymptotic concentration inequalities, which provide finite-sample guarantees under explicit assumptions.

Technical setup

Let:

\[ X_1,\ldots,X_n \]

be independent random variables satisfying:

\[ 0\leq X_i\leq1. \]

Let:

\[ \mu=\mathbb E[X_i] \]

and:

\[ \bar X_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i. \]

Hoeffding's inequality gives:

\[ P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\geq\epsilon)\leq2e^{-2n\epsilon^2}. \]

If the variance is known to satisfy:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X_i)\leq\sigma^2, \]

Bernstein's inequality gives:

\[ P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\geq\epsilon)\leq2\exp\left(-\frac{n\epsilon^2}{2\sigma^2+\frac23\epsilon}\right). \]

Research challenges

  1. Derive the sample-size requirement for Hoeffding's inequality to guarantee:

\[ P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\geq0.01)\leq0.05. \]

  1. Suppose the variance is known to be:

\[ \sigma^2=0.01. \]

Derive the corresponding sufficient sample-size requirement using Bernstein's inequality. 3. Compare the two bounds numerically. Why can knowledge of the variance produce a much tighter guarantee? 4. Explain the difference between a concentration inequality and a central limit theorem approximation. 5. What assumptions make Hoeffding's inequality valid? Why is boundedness important? 6. Define a sub-Gaussian random variable through its moment-generating function:

\[ \mathbb E[e^{t(X-\mathbb E[X])}]\leq e^{\sigma^2t^2/2}. \]

Explain why this condition is useful when analyzing random variables that are not necessarily bounded. 7. Compare Gaussian, sub-Gaussian, and sub-exponential tail behavior. 8. Suppose a model's errors are independent but have a heavy-tailed distribution with no finite variance. Why can the usual Bernstein inequality be inappropriate? 9. Discuss how clipping observations can produce bounded variables while changing the target expectation. 10. Explain why a statistically valid concentration guarantee may still be too conservative to be operationally useful. 11. Design an experiment comparing empirical confidence intervals based on Gaussian approximations, Hoeffding bounds, and Bernstein bounds. 12. Discuss how the distinction between asymptotic accuracy and finite-sample guarantees matters when evaluating small improvements in machine-learning systems.

Hints

  • The inequalities use different information about the distribution.
  • A theoretically valid upper bound is not necessarily close to the actual probability of error.

Exercise 05 — The Pairwise Metric That Uses the Same Observation Thousands of Times

Domain: U-statistics · ROC-AUC estimation · Hoeffding decomposition

Core intuition: Constructing thousands of pairwise comparisons from a small dataset does not create thousands of independent observations.

Scenario

A research team evaluates a binary classifier using ROC-AUC.

The dataset contains a relatively small number of positive and negative examples.

The evaluation procedure compares every positive observation with every negative observation.

The team celebrates because it has generated millions of pairwise comparisons.

However, many comparisons reuse the same individual observations.

You suspect the team is overestimating the amount of independent statistical information in its evaluation.

Technical setup

Let:

\[ X_1,\ldots,X_m \]

be positive-class observations and:

\[ Y_1,\ldots,Y_n \]

be negative-class observations.

Define:

\[ h(X_i,Y_j)=\mathbf1(s(X_i)>s(Y_j))+\frac12\mathbf1(s(X_i)=s(Y_j)), \]

where (s(\cdot)) is the classifier score.

The empirical AUC is:

\[ U_{m,n}=\frac1{mn}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}h(X_i,Y_j). \]

Let:

\[ \theta=\mathbb E[h(X,Y)]. \]

Define the first-order projections:

\[ h_1(x)=\mathbb E_Y[h(x,Y)]-\theta, \]

\[ h_2(y)=\mathbb E_X[h(X,y)]-\theta. \]

Research challenges

  1. Prove that the empirical AUC is an unbiased estimator of the population AUC:

\[ \mathbb E[U_{m,n}]=\theta. \]

  1. Consider two positive scores:

\[ X_1=0.8,\qquad X_2=0.6, \]

and two negative scores:

\[ Y_1=0.7,\qquad Y_2=0.4. \]

Calculate the empirical AUC. 3. Explain why the (mn) pairwise comparisons are not mutually independent. 4. Derive the first-order Hoeffding decomposition of the two-sample U-statistic. 5. Show that, under suitable non-degeneracy conditions:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(U_{m,n})\approx\frac{\operatorname{Var}(h_1(X))}{m}+\frac{\operatorname{Var}(h_2(Y))}{n}. \]

  1. Explain why the precision of the estimator depends separately on the number of positive and negative examples.
  2. What happens when one class is extremely rare, even if the total number of pairwise comparisons is large?
  3. Define a degenerate U-statistic and explain why the first-order approximation can fail in that case.
  4. Discuss why treating every pairwise comparison as an independent Bernoulli observation gives an incorrect standard error.
  5. Compare the U-statistic interpretation of AUC with ordinary sample-mean estimation.
  6. Design an evaluation procedure that estimates AUC uncertainty while respecting the dependence induced by shared observations.

Hints

  • Every positive observation participates in (n) comparisons.
  • Decompose each pairwise kernel into its overall expectation, its positive-observation contribution, its negative-observation contribution, and a residual interaction term.

Exercise 06 — The Average That Collapses When One Extreme Observation Appears

Domain: Robust statistics · Influence functions · Breakdown points · M-estimation

Core intuition: An estimator can be highly efficient under an ideal statistical model but catastrophically unstable under small amounts of contamination.

Scenario

A research laboratory analyzes the distribution of prediction errors produced by an AI system.

Most observations are small.

However, a few corrupted measurements contain extremely large values.

The ordinary mean changes dramatically after adding only one corrupted observation.

The team does not want to discard all large errors because some could represent genuine model failures.

You are asked to compare the robustness of different estimators.

Technical setup

Assume the uncontaminated population follows:

\[ X\sim\mathcal N(0,1). \]

Consider the contaminated distribution:

\[ P_\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)P+\varepsilon\delta_M, \]

where:

  • (\varepsilon) is the contamination fraction.
  • (\delta_M) is a point mass at an extreme value (M).

For an estimator (T(P)), define its influence function:

\[ \operatorname{IF}(z;T,P)=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow0}\frac{T((1-\varepsilon)P+\varepsilon\delta_z)-T(P)}{\varepsilon}. \]

Research challenges

  1. Derive the population mean under the contaminated distribution.
  2. Calculate the influence function of the sample mean functional.
  3. Explain why the mean has an unbounded influence function.
  4. Determine the population median under sufficiently small contamination at an extreme positive value.
  5. Derive the influence function of the median:

\[ \operatorname{IF}(z;\operatorname{Med},P)=\frac{\frac12-\mathbf1(z\leq m)}{f(m)}, \]

assuming a positive continuous density at the median (m). 6. Calculate its maximum absolute influence under the standard normal distribution. 7. Explain the breakdown point and compare the mean and median. 8. Consider the Huber score function:

\[ \psi_c(u)=\begin{cases}-c&u<-c,\u&|u|\leq c,\c&u>c.\end{cases} \]

Explain how an M-estimator based on this function limits the contribution of extreme observations. 9. For a known scale of one and the uncontaminated standard normal model, derive the influence function of the corresponding Huber location estimator. 10. Explain the trade-off between robustness and statistical efficiency under uncontaminated Gaussian data. 11. Why does automatically removing every extreme observation risk deleting genuine rare failures? 12. Design an experiment comparing the mean, median, and Huber estimator under several contamination fractions and contamination magnitudes.

Hints

  • Differentiate the estimator as a functional of the underlying probability distribution.
  • Robustness is about sensitivity to contamination, not simply the magnitude of the observed values.

Exercise 07 — The Model That Wins Against Every Other Model but Still Has Weak Statistical Evidence

Domain: Bayesian model evidence · Bayes factors · Laplace approximation · Occam's factor

Core intuition: Bayesian model comparison rewards good fit while penalizing models that spread prior probability over large regions of parameter space that the data do not support.

Scenario

A research group compares two probabilistic models.

Model A is a simple model with no adjustable parameter.

Model B introduces an additional parameter that can explain small deviations in the observations.

Model B achieves a better maximum likelihood.

The team immediately declares it superior.

You argue that evaluating only the best possible parameter value ignores how much prior parameter space the model allocated to explanations that fit the observations poorly.

You propose comparing marginal likelihoods instead.

Technical setup

Suppose:

\[ Y_i\mid\theta\sim\mathcal N(\theta,1). \]

Compare:

Model (M_0):

\[ \theta=0. \]

Model (M_1):

\[ \theta\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2),\qquad\tau^2=4. \]

The dataset contains:

\[ n=25 \]

observations with:

\[ \bar Y=0.4. \]

The Bayes factor is:

\[ BF_{10}=\frac{p(Y\mid M_1)}{p(Y\mid M_0)}. \]

Research challenges

  1. Calculate the maximum-likelihood estimate of (\theta) under (M_1).
  2. Compare the maximized likelihoods of (M_0) and (M_1).
  3. Derive the marginal likelihood:

\[ p(Y\mid M_1)=\int p(Y\mid\theta,M_1)p(\theta\mid M_1),d\theta. \]

  1. Use Gaussian conjugacy to derive the distribution of the sample mean under the two models.
  2. Show that the residual component of the likelihood cancels in the Bayes factor.
  3. Derive:

\[ BF_{10}=\sqrt{\frac{1/n}{\tau^2+1/n}}\exp\left[\frac{\bar Y^2}{2}\left(n-\frac{1}{\tau^2+1/n}\right)\right]. \]

  2. Explain why a model can have a larger maximum likelihood while receiving a smaller Bayes factor.
  3. Interpret the determinant or volume term appearing in the marginal likelihood as an Occam factor.

  6. Derive the general Laplace approximation:

Exercise 08 — One Infrastructure Failure That Creates Five More

Domain: Branching processes · Galton–Watson processes · Cascade probability

Core intuition: A failure process can become self-sustaining when each failure generates, on average, more than one additional failure.

Scenario

A large AI company operates a distributed computing cluster.

A server failure can overload neighboring servers.

An overloaded server may then fail, creating additional load on other machines.

The engineering team estimates the average number of secondary failures generated by one failed server.

They ask whether the cluster will eventually recover or whether an initial failure can trigger an unlimited cascade.

You propose modeling the process as a branching process.

Technical setup

Consider a Galton–Watson branching process.

Initially:

\[ Z_0=1. \]

Each failed unit independently produces a random number of additional failures.

Assume the number of offspring follows:

\[ N\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(m). \]

Thus:

\[ P(N=k)=e^{-m}\frac{m^k}{k!}. \]

Let (Z_t) denote the number of failures in generation (t).

The probability-generating function is:

\[ G(s)=\mathbb E[s^N]. \]

The extinction probability (q) satisfies:

\[ q=G(q). \]

Research challenges

  1. Derive the probability-generating function for the Poisson offspring distribution.
  2. Establish the recursive relationship between the generation sizes.
  3. Derive the equation:

\[ q=e^{m(q-1)}. \]

  1. Explain why (q=1) is always a solution.

  2. Show that:

    • If (m\leq1), extinction occurs with probability one under the usual non-degeneracy conditions.
    • If (m>1), extinction has probability less than one.

  3. For (m=0.8), derive the expected total number of failures across all generations.

  4. Calculate that expected total.

  5. Explain why an expected finite cascade size does not imply that every cascade is small.

  6. For (m=1.2), explain why the extinction probability satisfies a nontrivial fixed-point equation.

  7. Estimate the smaller solution of:

\[ q=e^{1.2(q-1)}. \]

  1. Suppose an engineering intervention reduces the average number of secondary failures per failed server from 1.2 to 0.9. Analyze how fundamentally this changes system reliability.
  2. Discuss the limitations of the independent-offspring assumption when failures occur in a network with heterogeneous connectivity and shared environmental conditions.
  3. Design an experiment to estimate the effective reproduction number of cascading failures in a distributed AI system.

Hints

  • Extinction probability is determined by a fixed point of the offspring generating function.
  • The expected total cascade size and the probability of eventual extinction are related but distinct quantities.

Exercise 09 — Detecting a Distribution Shift Without Knowing What the Correct Distribution Should Look Like

Domain: Maximum Mean Discrepancy · Kernel two-sample testing · Permutation inference

Core intuition: Two distributions can be compared through their mean embeddings in a reproducing-kernel Hilbert space, allowing statistical testing without explicitly estimating their probability densities.

Scenario

An AI company deploys a model in two environments.

Environment A represents the original training distribution.

Environment B represents live production traffic.

The model performs well initially, but the engineers suspect that the input distribution has changed.

They do not know the correct parametric distribution for either environment.

They need a statistical test that determines whether the two sample populations differ.

You propose maximum mean discrepancy.

Technical setup

Let:

\[ X_1,\ldots,X_m\sim P \]

and:

\[ Y_1,\ldots,Y_n\sim Q. \]

Let (k) be a positive-definite kernel with feature map:

\[ \phi(x) \]

into a reproducing-kernel Hilbert space.

Define the kernel mean embeddings:

\[ \mu_P=\mathbb E_P[\phi(X)], \]

\[ \mu_Q=\mathbb E_Q[\phi(Y)]. \]

Maximum mean discrepancy is:

\[ \operatorname{MMD}(P,Q)=|\mu_P-\mu_Q|_{\mathcal H}. \]

Its squared population form is:

\[ \operatorname{MMD}^2(P,Q)=\mathbb E[k(X,X')]+\mathbb E[k(Y,Y')]-2\mathbb E[k(X,Y)]. \]

Research challenges

  1. Derive the population MMD expression from the squared Hilbert-space norm.
  2. Explain why MMD compares distributions through their expected feature representations.
  3. Derive the unbiased empirical estimator:

\[ \widehat{\operatorname{MMD}}_u^2=\frac{1}{m(m-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}k(X_i,X_j)+\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\neq j}k(Y_i,Y_j)-\frac{2}{mn}\sum_{i,j}k(X_i,Y_j). \]

  1. Explain why this unbiased estimator can be negative in finite samples even though the population MMD squared cannot be negative.
  2. Distinguish characteristic kernels from kernels that fail to uniquely identify distributions through their mean embeddings.
  3. Why is a Gaussian RBF kernel characteristic under the standard Euclidean-domain assumptions?
  4. Explain how the kernel bandwidth influences the scale of differences the test is sensitive to.
  5. Suppose the two environments have identical means but different variances. Explain why a linear kernel may fail to detect their difference while a suitable nonlinear kernel can succeed.
  6. Derive the permutation-testing procedure under the null hypothesis:

\[ H_0:P=Q. \]

Why is exchangeability important? 10. Explain why a permutation test can provide a valid nonparametric significance assessment without specifying the distribution of the observations. 11. What problems arise when the kernel bandwidth is selected using the same observed samples and is not accounted for in the test calibration? 12. Design an MMD-based monitoring strategy for detecting distribution changes in production AI systems while controlling false alarms across repeated monitoring windows.

Hints

  • The mean embedding is an expectation of feature vectors, not an expectation of the raw observations alone.
  • Under the null hypothesis, the labels indicating which sample came from which distribution are exchangeable.

Exercise 10 — Finding an Unknown Root Using Only Noisy Observations

Domain: Stochastic approximation · Robbins–Monro algorithm · Convergence conditions

Core intuition: A stochastic algorithm can approach an unknown solution without ever observing the exact function it is trying to solve.

Scenario

A machine-learning laboratory develops an adaptive sensor-calibration system.

The ideal calibration parameter is defined as the root of a function representing the expected sensor response.

However, each evaluation of the function is noisy because the sensor is affected by random environmental conditions.

The engineering team cannot calculate the exact expected function.

They only receive noisy measurements.

You are asked to design an iterative procedure that finds the required parameter using these observations.

Technical setup

Consider finding a solution to:

\[ g(\theta)=0. \]

Suppose the available observation is:

\[ H(\theta,\xi), \]

where:

\[ \mathbb E[H(\theta,\xi)]=g(\theta). \]

Consider the update:

\[ \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-a_tH(\theta_t,\xi_{t+1}). \]

For the concrete example, suppose:

\[ g(\theta)=\theta^2-2, \]

and:

\[ H(\theta,\xi)=\theta^2-2+\xi, \]

where:

\[ \mathbb E[\xi]=0,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\xi)=\sigma^2<\infty. \]

The positive root is:

\[ \theta^*=\sqrt2. \]

Consider step sizes:

\[ a_t=\frac{a}{t+1}. \]

Research challenges

  1. Verify that the noisy observation is an unbiased estimate of the root function.
  2. Write the explicit stochastic update.
  3. Explain why the algorithm moves toward a root when its expected drift points in the appropriate direction.
  4. Linearize the expected update around the positive root and investigate local stability.
  5. Calculate:

\[ g'(\theta^*). \]

What does its sign imply? 6. Compare a constant step size:

\[ a_t=a \]

with a decreasing step size.

\[ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}a_t=\infty, \]

  1. Verify whether the proposed harmonic step-size schedule satisfies both conditions.
  2. Explain why a step-size sequence that decreases too quickly may prevent the algorithm from fully correcting an initial estimation error.
  3. Discuss the assumptions on the noise process that are needed to establish convergence.

Final research standard

Every solution should contain four distinct layers.

1. Probability foundations

Can you reason correctly about conditional expectation, dependence, distributions, and stochastic behavior?

2. Statistical structure

Can you identify sufficient information, estimator properties, dependence mechanisms, and statistical limitations?

3. Mathematical investigation

Can you derive the relevant identities, establish convergence or error guarantees, and construct counterexamples?

4. Research application

Can you use the mathematical result to improve an AI experiment, diagnose a model, design an algorithm, or challenge an existing methodology?

The final challenge

A researcher should be able to look at a sophisticated AI system and question:

  • Is there a more informative statistic hidden in the data?
  • Are our observations independent, or merely uncorrelated?
  • Are two systems genuinely independent in their extreme failures?
  • Does our estimator exploit all available information?
  • Can a few corrupted observations dominate our conclusions?
  • Does our evidence support the model itself or only a particular parameter configuration?
  • Can we discover distributional differences without assuming a parametric family?
  • Can an algorithm converge when the underlying function is available only through noisy measurements?

The goal is not to become someone who knows hundreds of statistical methods. It is to become someone who understands the mathematical conditions under which those methods deserve to be trusted.
